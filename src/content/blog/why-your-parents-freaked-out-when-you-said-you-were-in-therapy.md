You finally worked up the courage to tell them.

Maybe you sent a text. Maybe you had the conversation face-to-face. Maybe you blurted it out during a video call and immediately regretted it.

Their reaction was probably one of these:

·       Panic. Full-blown"something is wrong with our child" panic.

·       Silence. The kind that makesyou want to take it back immediately.

·       Questions. Lots of them. All of them feeling accusatory.

·       Dismissal. "You don't need therapy. You just need to think positive/ work harder/ pray more."

·       Blame. Either of themselves ("What did we do wrong?") or of you ("You're overthinking things").

And now you're confused. You told them you were taking care of yourself. You were trying to be responsible. So why did they react like you'd just told them you were in crisis?

Because to them, you kind of did.

## What Your Parents Heard When You Said Therapy

When you say "I'm in therapy,"your parents don't hear "I'm doing something healthy for myself."

They hear one of these things: "Something is deeply wrong with you"

In your parents' generation and culture, therapy meant one thing: you were broken. You were unstable. You were the "crazy one." Mental health wasn't something you worked on preventively- it was something you admitted when you'd hit rock bottom.

So when you casually say "I'm seeing a therapist," their brain translates it as: My child is in crisis and didn't tell me.

"We failed as parents"

Your parents may instantly blame themselves. "If our child needs therapy, we must have done something wrong." They're searching their memory for the moment they failed you. They're wondering if they pushed too hard, or didn't push hard enough. If they were too strict, or too lenient. If they should have seen this coming. This is their fear talking, not reality. But it's real for them.

"You'rerejecting our way of handling things"

In many Indian families, therapy represents a rejection of traditional wisdom. It's saying: "Your advice isn't enough. Your way of handling emotions isn't good enough. We need an outsider to fix what you couldn't." Even if you didn't mean it that way, that's what they might hear.

"Something must be very wrong to make you do this"

Your parents may jump to conclusions aboutwhat's happening. Are you depressed? Are you in a bad relationship? Are youdoing drugs? Are you suicidal? Their minds go to the worst-case scenariobecause that's the only framework they have for understanding therapy.

## Why They're Reacting, Not Supporting

Here's what's important to understand: Their reaction isn't actually about you. It's about their fear, their worldview, and their love. Your parents are scared. Not angry but scared.

They're scared because:

·       In their world, therapy =serious crisis

·       You didn't tell them you were struggling (which makes them feel like they failed to notice)

·       They don't understand what therapy is or does

·       They're worried it's going to change you

·       They're worried you're going totell the therapist things about them

·       They're worried what people will think if they find out

·       They love you and they'reterrified something is seriously wrong

This doesn't mean:

·       They don't support you

·       They don't love you

·       They won't eventually come around

·       You should hide therapy from them

It means they need time. And context. And a different way of understanding what you're doing.

## The First Few Days (And Why They're Hard)

Right after you tell them, things often get worse before they get better.

 Day1: Panic. Questions. Maybe they ask for details about what you told the therapist. Maybe they ask who referred you. Maybe they want to meet the therapist.

Day2-3: Silence. They're processing. They might be talking to each other (if both parents), or to trusted family members. They might be Googling "therapy for young adults." They might be blaming themselves.

Day4-7: Negotiation. They might suggest alternatives: "Why don't you just talk to your dad?" or "Have you tried meditation?" or "Maybe you should visit home for a week to feel better." This is them trying to offer solutions that fit their worldview.

Week 2: Reality starts to sink in. They realize you're serious about this. They might start asking genuine questions: "What do you talk about?" "Is it helping?" "How much does it cost?" These are actually good signs.

Week3-4: Acceptance (sometimes). Or continued resistance (sometimes). Most parents land somewhere in the middle: they accept you're doing it, but they don't fully understand it or support it.

## What Will Actually Help Them Come Around

1.Let them know you're not in crisis

Sometimes the simple statement helps: "I'm not struggling with anything serious right now. I'm doing this because I want to handle stress better before it becomes a problem. It's preventive, not reactive." This reframes therapy from "emergency" to "maintenance."

2.Give them a reason that makes sense in their worldview

Your parents understand performance. They understand optimization. They understand investing in your future. So instead of: "I'm dealing with anxiety and guilt" Try: "I realized I want to be more effective at work/studies, and a therapist helps me think more clearly." This isn't lying. It's translating your needs into a language they understand.

3.Show results

The fastest way to get your parents onboard is to show improvement. Better sleep? Tell them. More focus on work? Mention it. Less reactive? They'll notice. When they see concrete changes, therapy stops being an abstract concern and becomes "oh, this is actually helping."

4.Invite them into the process (carefully)

Some therapists are happy to do a brief family session or send a note to your parents explaining what therapy is and why it's helpful. This isn't about telling them your secrets—it's about demystifying the process. You could say: "Would you be open to a family call with my therapist just to understand what we're doing?" This shows confidence in what you're doing and removes the mystery.

5.Be patient with them

This is hard, but it matters: your parents are also learning. They didn't grow up with therapy. They didn't talk about mental health. They learned to handle emotions by pushing through, not by processing. Give them time to adjust to this new thing.

## What NOT to Do

·       Don't hide therapy from them(if they ask directly, be honest- lies erode trust)

·       Don't use therapy to criticize them (therapists aren't there to blame parents; they're there to help you process family dynamics)

·       Don't expect them to understand immediately (they won't, and that's okay)

·       Don't argue about whether therapy is necessary (if it's helping you, that's what matters)

·       Don't overshare what you discuss in therapy (keeps boundaries healthy, and privacy actually reassures them that therapy is legitimate)

·       Don't expect them to change based on what you're learning (therapy is about you, not fixing your parents)

## After the Initial Panic: What Usually Happens

Here's the realistic timeline:

Weeks1-2: Shock, questions, possibly anger or sadness

Weeks3-4: Curiosity. They start asking how it's going,what you talk about

Months1-3: Acceptance (begrudging or genuine). They stop actively opposing it

Months3+: Some parents become your biggest supporters.They might even open up about their own struggles. Others stay neutral butaccepting.

A few months later: You might hear them casually mention it to relatives: "Our daughter is seeing a therapist and it's helping her." Suddenly, therapy is normalized.

## The Bigger Picture

Your parents' freak-out isn't really about therapy. It's about: Their fear that you're suffering (and they didn't know). Their discomfort with emotions being discussed professionally. Their generational worldview about mental health. Their love for you, expressed as panic.

None of this means you shouldn't be in therapy. It just means the conversation requires patience, context, and grace- for both of you.

You're doing the right thing.

Taking care of your mental health is mature. Processing your emotions with a professional is healthy. Setting this boundary with your parents- therapy is something you're doing for you- is actually a sign of strength.

Their reaction doesn't change that.

## A Note: If Their Reaction Is Harmful

If your parents respond by:

·       Threatening to cut you off financially

·       Isolating you from family

·       Telling you to stop therapy (and you're an adult)

·       Using it as ammunition inarguments

·       Spreading it to relatives as gossip/shame

This is a sign that their response is about control, not care. This is worth processing in therapy. Your therapist can help you set boundaries with your parents while maintaining your own mental health. You don't need their permission to take care of yourself. And if their response is harmful, you have the right to protect your own wellbeing.

## Moving Forward

Your parents will eventually adjust. Some faster, some slower. But what matters right now is this:

You did something brave- You recognized you needed support and you took action. That takes courage. Their panic is temporary and your commitment to your mental health is permanent. And slowly, over time, you'll probably find that their panic turns into understanding. Until then, be patient with them. And be patient with yourself.
