In many northern cities it is dark by half past four. You wake up to a grey sky, drag yourself through the day, and by evening the idea of seeing anyone feels like too much. You tell yourself it is the cold, or the workload, or just being far from home.

For some people, that heaviness is more than a bad week. It follows the seasons, comes back each winter and lifts in spring. This is called seasonal depression, or seasonal affective disorder (SAD), and it is easy to miss in your first winter abroad, when so much else is new as well.

This post explains how to tell winter blues from seasonal depression, why a first winter in a new country can hit harder, and which CBT and ACT-informed habits can help. It also covers when it is worth talking to a doctor or counsellor.

## Winter blues or seasonal depression?

The difference is mostly about how strong the symptoms are and how much they get in the way of daily life.

Winter blues are common. Shorter days can leave you slower, a little flat and less keen to go out, but you can still work, see people and enjoy things once you do.

Seasonal depression is a form of depression that follows a seasonal pattern. Episodes usually begin in autumn or winter, ease in spring or summer, and often return in the same way each year. The symptoms are similar to other depression and can include:

* Low mood for most of the day, nearly every day
* Losing interest in things you normally enjoy
* Low energy and a heavy, slowed-down feeling
* Sleeping much more than usual and still feeling tired
* Strong cravings for carbohydrates, or changes in weight
* Trouble concentrating
* Withdrawing from people
* Feeling hopeless or worthless

This is general information, not a diagnosis. Only a doctor or mental health professional can tell you which applies to you.

A useful check: if the low lasts more than two weeks, if it interferes with work or relationships, or if it has returned over more than one winter, it is worth getting assessed rather than waiting for spring.

## Why a first winter abroad can hit harder

A new winter lands on top of everything else that is new.

* **Your body is adjusting to less light.**

   If you grew up somewhere with bright mornings and long days, a sudden drop in daylight is a real change. Reduced light is thought to affect sleep, body-clock rhythms and mood, which is why light is central to how SAD is treated.
* **Your routines are not built yet.**

   At home, habits and people hold you up without any effort. In a new place you are still working out where to shop, who to call and what a good weekend looks like, so there is less to catch you when your energy drops.
* **Your support is far away.**

   Time differences put family calls at awkward hours, and the people nearby may not yet be people you would call at the end of a bad day.
* **The cold keeps you indoors.**

   Staying in cuts light, movement and contact, which are the very things that protect mood.
* **Everything looks like homesickness.**

   It is easy to put the low down to missing home, which is partly true, and to miss the part that follows a seasonal pattern.

Many people find their first winters abroad the hardest, because the body and the routines are still adjusting. That is a good reason to plan ahead rather than wait and see.

## Why it is easy to brush off

Many people explain away a seasonal low for weeks, or even years.

Common explanations include "I'm just tired", "I'm lazy in winter", "everyone gets this" and "I should be grateful to be here". If you are managing work or studies, or are used to coping on your own, a low can stay hidden for a long time because you are still functioning on the surface.

For some people from Indian backgrounds there can be another layer. Depression is sometimes spoken of as weakness, as something for people who "have a reason to be sad", or as something to fix by staying busy. If you moved abroad for an opportunity, it can feel as though you have no right to struggle.

Depression is not a measure of gratitude or character. A mood that follows the seasons is a pattern, and patterns can be understood and treated. Noticing it early makes it much easier to manage.

## What can help

Most people do best with a mix of practical habits and, where needed, professional support.

**Habits to build**

* **Get light early.**

   Spend time outdoors in daylight, ideally in the morning, even when it is cloudy, and sit near windows indoors.
* **Use behavioural activation.**

   When mood drops, activity drops, and that lowers mood further. Schedule small, specific activities each day, such as a walk, a call or cooking something you like, and do them even if you do not feel like it first. Mood often follows action.
* **Protect sleep and meals.**

   Keep regular wake-up and bed times and eat at regular hours. Oversleeping can feel comforting but often deepens the heaviness.
* **Move your body.**

   Even a 15 to 20 minute walk helps.
* **Plan social contact.**

   Put one or two regular plans in the calendar, such as a weekly dinner or a class, so you do not have to find the energy each time.
* **Notice the thoughts.**

   CBT helps you spot thoughts like "this will never lift" or "I'm just lazy" and test them against the evidence. ACT adds the idea of seeing a thought as a thought while still acting on what matters.
* **Make room for the feeling.**

   You do not have to feel better before you act. ACT calls this willingness: letting the heaviness be there while you take one small step.

**Medical options to ask about**

Some people use light therapy, which involves sitting in front of a special bright light box in the morning, or medication. There is also a form of CBT designed for seasonal depression. These need a doctor's guidance, and light boxes in particular are not suitable for everyone, so talk to a doctor before starting. A doctor can also advise on whether to check your vitamin D levels.

## When to seek extra support

Seasonal depression often responds to treatment, and you do not have to wait for spring. It is worth talking to a doctor or counsellor if:

* Your mood has been low or flat for more than two weeks.
* Your sleep, appetite, energy or concentration are clearly affected.
* You are withdrawing from friends, work or things you used to enjoy.
* The same pattern has come back over more than one winter.
* You feel hopeless, or you have thoughts of harming yourself.

If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, please contact a local emergency service or crisis helpline straight away.
