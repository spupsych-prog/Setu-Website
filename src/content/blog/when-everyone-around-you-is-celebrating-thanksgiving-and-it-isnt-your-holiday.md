The festive menus go up, colleagues' out-of-office messages fill your inbox, and the shop windows change overnight. Everyone seems to know exactly what they are doing for the next five weeks. If these holidays are not yours, it can feel like standing just outside a room where a party is going on.

That feeling is more common than people admit, and it rarely has a single cause. It can be homesickness, a small sense of being left out, or the quiet recognition that your own festivals pass without anyone around you noticing.

This post looks at why the festive season can feel lonely when it is not your holiday, how comparison keeps the feeling going., and how a few habits can help you make these weeks your own.

## Why the season hits harder when the holiday is not yours

Holidays amplify feelings about belonging, and when the holiday is not yours you can feel both inside it and outside it at once.

Everyday life gives you plenty of ways to feel part of things: work, routines, small talk. The festive season switches many of those off. Offices slow down, friends travel to their families, and plans are made around traditions you did not grow up with. What is left is unstructured time, which is exactly when thoughts about home, family and who you would normally be with tend to get louder.

There is also a quieter layer. Many people from Indian backgrounds are used to festivals as shared, noisy, full-house events. A holiday that is mostly a single dinner or a quiet day off can feel strangely small, even when you are invited. Being included and feeling that you belong are not the same thing.

None of this means you are ungrateful or that something is wrong with you. It is a normal response to a season built around belonging to a particular place.

## The comparison trap

Comparison is what turns a quiet season into a lonely one.

Social media makes it easy. Feeds fill with family tables, matching outfits and countdowns to reunions, and each image sits next to your own ordinary evening. The mind then draws a conclusion that is rarely fair: everyone else has somewhere to be, and I do not.

You may also hear "What are you doing for the holidays?" more often, and feel pressure to give an answer that sounds full. The wish to look occupied can lead to saying yes to plans you do not want, or saying "nothing much" in a flat voice that leaves you feeling worse.

A few things worth remembering:

* **Feeds show highlights.**

   Many of the people posting have lonely or difficult moments of their own, and those are rarely shared.
* **Thoughts are not facts.**

   "Everyone else has someone" is a thought. You can notice it and ask what supports it and what does not, without having to argue yourself out of the feeling.
* **A short answer is enough.**

   "Keeping it quiet and calling family" is a perfectly good reply and usually ends the conversation kindly.

## When your own festivals pass quietly

Some of the loneliest moments are the ones nobody around you knows about.

Diwali fell in early November this year, and for many people abroad it landed on a working day or was squeezed into a weekend. Colleagues may not have known it was happening. Your family may have celebrated without you, with the sound of it reaching you only through a video call. Other festivals through the year can pass in the same way.

That can bring up something easy to miss: missing a version of the season that you had and now do not. It is not only homesickness. It is also missing the sense of being noticed and of being part of something shared.

Two things can help. First, name it for what it is. "I am missing something that mattered to me" is more accurate, and kinder, than "I am being dramatic." Second, tell one person. A colleague or friend who hears "that was my big festival and it felt quiet" often responds warmly, and sometimes asks to join in next time.

## Making room for your own traditions

You do not have to choose between your holidays and the ones around you; you can build a small mix of both.

Traditions do not have to be big to count. What matters is that they are repeatable and meaningful to you.

* **Borrow one.**

   Accept an invitation to a local holiday meal, or watch a tradition as a guest rather than someone who has to feel it fully. Curiosity is enough.
* **Bring one of yours.**

   Cook a dish from home for colleagues, light a diya, or play the music that fills your family's house at this time of year. Sharing something of yours often starts the conversations that build connection.
* **Create a new one.**

   Many people abroad end up with a small ritual of their own: a long call with family on a fixed day, a walk on the morning of the holiday, dinner with friends who are also far from home.
* **Invite someone.**

   Others in your city may also be spending the holidays without family. A simple "I'm cooking, come over" or "want to get dinner on the 25th?" can be a relief for both of you.

Start small and keep it realistic. One tradition you actually do is worth more than a plan for five that you drop.

## A CBT and ACT toolkit for the holiday weeks

Small, specific habits work better than trying to force a cheerful mood.

* **Spot the comparison thought.**

   When you catch yourself thinking "everyone else has plans", write it down and add one piece of evidence that does not fit. CBT calls this testing the thought.
* **Clarify what matters to you.**

   ACT starts with values. Ask yourself, "What would make these weeks feel like mine?" It might be a long call with family, rest, or a small celebration. Choose one action that matches it.
* **Plan the empty stretches.**

   Unstructured days are where low mood tends to grow. Put one small, pleasant activity in each, such as a walk, a film or a meal you enjoy cooking. This is behavioural activation.
* **Limit the scroll.**

   Notice how you feel after 15 minutes on social media. If it leaves you lower, set a time limit or mute some accounts for the season.
* **Make room for the feeling.**

   You can feel homesick and still go to the dinner. ACT calls this willingness: letting the feeling be there while you act on what matters.
* **Reach out first.**

   Send the message you hoped someone would send you. People are often relieved to be asked.

## When to seek extra support

A few wistful evenings are normal, but some signs suggest it is worth talking to someone.

* Low mood or loss of interest that lasts more than two weeks.
* Trouble sleeping, eating or concentrating that does not settle.
* Pulling away from people more and more, or dreading this season each year.
* A constant sense of being on the outside, even when you are with others.

A counsellor can help you work out what belongs to this season and what has been building for longer. If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, please contact a local emergency service or crisis helpline straight away.
