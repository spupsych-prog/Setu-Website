t's a Tuesday evening in your seventh year abroad. The flat is quiet, work is going well enough, and a college friend has just sent photos from a wedding in Bengaluru that you couldn't attend. You close the app and open a different tab: *cost of living, Bengaluru vs. Seattle*. Nobody is ill. Nothing has gone wrong. You simply can't stop asking the same question: should I move back to India?

If that sounds familiar, you are far from alone. The question surfaces again and again in forums, group chats and long phone calls between friends who left around the same time. It rarely has one cause, and it rarely resolves with one insight.

This guide is a psychologist's way of working through it: why the question loops, what is actually pulling in each direction, and how to decide in a way you can stand behind.

## Why this question has no clean answer

The question stays open because both options are real and neither is free. Staying and returning each cost something and give something, but the costs and gains come in different currencies: salary against closeness, security against belonging, momentum against ease. There is no common unit to compare them in, so the mind keeps recalculating.

Two things tighten the loop for people who moved by choice. First, the move was often meant to be open-ended, so there was never a decision point; the question simply accumulated year by year. Second, almost every input is uncertain: how your career will hold up, how a relationship will develop, how India will feel after years away. A decision with no deadline and unreliable information is exactly the kind the mind circles, because circling feels like working on it.

It is also not only a practical question.Moving back says something about who you are and where you belong, which is whya list of pros and cons rarely settles it.

## What is actually pulling each way

"Move back or stay" is not onedecision. It is several decisions bundled together, and separating them is thefirst useful step.

Deciding whether to move back to India rarely comes down to one thing. It's usually eight smaller questions pulling in different directions. On career, India may offer roles, sectors or ventures within reach and the pull of a growing market, while the seniority you've built over years, your specialised work and a track record that travels can hold you where you are. So it helps to ask which role, in which city, you would take if you moved next month. On money, lower living costs in some cities and the chance to build in your own market can draw you back, but a savings buffer you've already built and the cost of resetting argue for staying. A useful question is how many months of runway would make you feel steady, and whether you have them. Friendships pull both ways too: old friends, a shared language and the ease of belonging on one side, and the community you've built here on the other, along with the fact that friends in India now have full lives of their own. Ask yourself who, specifically, you would see every week. In your partnership, family and community around the relationship and your partner's wishes may point towards India, while your partner's work, residency or roots, and a relationship built here, may point the other way. The most honest question is what your partner wants and whether the two of you have said it out loud. Belonging and identity can feel like a pull towards being understood without explaining yourself, yet distance from scrutiny can also leave you feeling freer and more like yourself. It's worth asking where you feel most like yourself on an ordinary Tuesday. Security matters as well: not being tied to visas or renewals is a real draw, but residency, status and the ability to leave later are what keep your options open. Ask which door you could reopen later and which one closes when you decide. Daily life brings its own weight, with food, pace, household help and family nearby on one side and the systems and routines you've adapted to on the other, so ask which daily frictions you're willing to live with for ten years. Finally, parents and family can draw you back through shared ordinary life, such as dinners, festivals and simply being around. If your parents are healthy and independent, distance is not a crisis, which makes the better question what kind of presence each of you actually wants.

## Why the decision gets stuck

Four well-understood mental habits keep this decision looping.

Rumination feels like progress. Going over the same question in the same abstract way ("what if I regret it?") produces the sensation of working on the problem without producing a new fact or a next step. A simple test: has this round of thinking given me information or an action? If not, it is circling, not deciding.

We mispredict how we will feel. Research on affective forecasting (Wilson and Gilbert, 2005) finds that people tend to overestimate both the intensity and the duration of their emotional reactions to future events. One cause is focalism: when you picture your first month back in India, or your tenth winter abroad, you imagine only that one thing and forget the deadlines, friendships and routines that will fill the same weeks. Another is that people fail to anticipate how quickly they will make sense of a new situation and adapt. This cuts both ways. The homecoming you imagine may feel less magical than you expect, and the disruption you dread may feel less overwhelming.

The choice is framed as all or nothing. "Move back for good" versus "stay for good" hides a range in between: working from India for an extended stretch, a defined trial period, a return with a set exit plan, or staying with a rhythm of long visits home.

## The CBT lens: the thoughts that keep the loop running

In cognitive behavioural therapy, the first step is to catch the thought driving the feeling, then test it against evidence instead of arguing with it. Three thoughts often run in the background of this decision:

•      "If I stay, I will have missed my chance to build a life at home." (fortune-telling, all-or-nothing thinking)

•      "If I go back and it does not work, I will have failed twice." (catastrophising)

•      "A good decision would feel certain." (demanding certainty, then reading its absence as proof of a mistake)

A balanced thought does not settle where you should live. It lowers the temperature so you can think about the question clearly. Thoughts that cannot be checked against evidence, such as "what if I regret it?", are better handled with the tools in the next section.

## The ACT lens: values, not verdicts

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)swaps the question "which choice is right?" for "which choice moves me toward what matters, and can I carry its costs?"

Values are not goals. In ACT, values are chosen qualities of how you want to act, such as connection, curiosity, contribution or security. You live them moment by moment, and they are never finished. Goals are concrete and can be achieved: "be in Pune by March", "get permanent residency". Many people argue about goals (India versus abroad)when the real tension is between two values, such as belonging and growth. Name the values first, then ask which goals serve them.

Defuse from the thought without obeying it. Try labelling it: "I am having the thought that I am running out of time." The change is small, but it reduces how believable and sticky the thought feels, without arguing with it or trying to make it disappear.

Ask what is workable. For any option, and for any habit of thinking, ask whether it moves you toward the life you want or only reduces discomfort today. Researching salaries in Pune is workable. Refreshing property listings at midnight probably is not.

Choose the discomfort you are willing to carry. Both paths involve some. Psychological flexibility, the central aim of ACT, is the ability to persist with or change a behaviour depending on whether it serves your chosen values. You may not find a path without loss. You can choose the loss you are prepared to carry for something you care about.

Acton your values from where you are. Connection can live in a weekly dinner call, in shared rituals, in showing up for a friend's milestone, not only in a postcode. The same holds in reverse: if you return, building a new life in India, rather than only rejoining the old one, is committed action too.

## A way to decide

You can turn a looping question into a process with a deadline. These six steps do not require you to feel certain first.

1.     Write two specific lives. For each option, describe an ordinary Tuesday eighteen months from now: the city, the work, who you see, what you eat, what irritates you. Specifics deflate both fantasy and dread.

2.     Rank the factors. Mark the two or three factors that matter most to you. If belonging outranks seniority, or the reverse, that is information.

3.     Sort fear from information. For each worry, ask whether it is a fact you can look up (salary ranges, rent, visa routes) or a prediction about how you will feel. Research the first. Give the second a time limit, remembering that forecasts of feeling tend to run too intense and too long.

## Whichever way you go

Both choices come with an adjustment period that people tend to underestimate.

If you return. Coming home after living overseas typically involves a period of adjustment known as re-entry shock, or reverse culture shock. It reflects not only what has changed at home but also how much you have changed while away ([https://oiss.yale.edu/campus-community-life/for-students/practical-matters/leaving-yale/returning-home). ](https://oiss.yale.edu/campus-community-life/for-students/practical-matters/leaving-yale/returning-home)It varies widely: some people feel little, while others take months or longer to settle. A common pattern is a honeymoon of reunions and excitement, then harder feelings once the novelty fades. These can include judging the home culture harshly, feeling that you have changed and may not fit in, feeling overwhelmed by starting over, or wondering whether returning was a mistake. Two things help: knowing the pattern before it arrives, and using the coping skills you already used when you first moved abroad. Talking to people who have returned also helps. Plan the first ninety days on purpose: routines, one or two committed social anchors, and a review point before you judge the move.

If you stay. The risk is a life held in "not yet": friendships kept shallow in case you leave, and no routine that is truly yours. That quiet waiting can wear you down. Whatever you decide, build as though you will be here for the next five years. Invest in a few local friendships, keep a weekly rhythm, and put something of your own into the place where you live. You can still leave later, and you will have lived in the meantime.

## When it is more than a decision

Sometimes the circling is a symptom rather than a decision problem, and that deserves proper support. Signs worth taking seriously:

•      low mood on most days for morethan a couple of weeks

•      changes in sleep or appetite,or constant exhaustion

•      losing interest in things youusually enjoy

•      anxiety or panic thatinterferes with work or relationships

•      feeling hopeless, or thinkingthat others would be better off without you

If these fit, speak with a qualified mentalhealth professional, in the country where you live or online.

If you are thinking about harming yourself,or you are worried about your safety, contact local emergency services or acrisis line now rather than waiting.
