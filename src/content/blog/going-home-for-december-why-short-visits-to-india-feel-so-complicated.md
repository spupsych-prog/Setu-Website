The tickets are booked, the suitcase is half full of gifts and favourite snacks, and somewhere between excitement and dread you are already counting how few days you will have. Going home for a short visit is one of the most anticipated and least discussed parts of living abroad.

It is easy to assume a trip home should feel simple: you see your family, eat the food you miss and recharge. For many people it is more mixed than that. You may feel completely at ease on day one, oddly out of place by day five, and quietly low on the flight back.

This post looks at why short visits to India carry so much emotional weight, what is usually going on underneath, and how a few CBT and ACT-informed habits can help you get more out of the trip and recover more gently from it.

## Why a short visit carries so much weight

A two-week trip has to hold a year of missing people, so every day starts to feel like it should count for more than a day.

When you live abroad, home is no longer a place you pass through on weekends. It becomes a limited resource. You may have a mental list of everyone to see, every dish to eat and every conversation you have been saving up. Your family may have their own list for you. Add jet lag, wedding invitations and relatives who are only in town this week, and the schedule fills up before you have unpacked.

Two things tend to happen. First, expectations rise: a trip that has to be special leaves little room for an ordinary, tired or irritable day. Second, ordinary moments start to feel like evidence. If a conversation feels flat, or you want an hour alone, the mind can quickly turn it into "I am wasting the little time I have" or "something has changed between us". In CBT terms, when the stakes feel high we are more likely to read neutral moments as problems.

None of this means the trip is going badly. It usually means the trip matters a great deal.

## Feeling like a guest in your own home

Many people are surprised to feel like a visitor in the city where they grew up.

Life at home kept moving while you were away. A favourite café has closed, the roads have changed, your friends have new routines, and your family has habits you were not part of. Your old room may now double as a storeroom or a guest room. You have changed too: you may be more independent, more direct or used to a different pace, and your preferences may no longer fit neatly into the household routine.

People in this position often describe belonging in two places and fully in neither. That is common for anyone who lives between cultures, and it is not a sign that you have "lost" home. It is what happens when two real lives exist side by side.

A helpful idea from ACT is to notice the thought "I don't belong here anymore" as a thought, rather than a verdict. Belonging is not all or nothing. It is built in small moments, such as cooking with a parent, meeting an old friend or walking a familiar street, even while part of you still feels like a visitor.

## The questions you have heard before

"So when are you moving back?" is rarely just a question.

Short visits tend to bring the same set of questions: when are you coming back for good, when will you settle down, why do you live so far away, is the job really worth it. They are usually asked out of love, curiosity or a wish to keep you close, and often in front of relatives who have their own views.

Even so, they can land as pressure. You may feel the need to justify your life abroad, defend a decision you are still unsure about, or say what people want to hear to keep the peace. The cost is that you leave feeling you spent the trip performing rather than connecting.

A few approaches that help:

* **Decide your short answer in advance.**

   One or two warm, true sentences, such as "We're happy there for now, and we'll keep thinking about it", is enough. You do not owe a full defence at every dinner.
* **Hear the worry behind the question.**

   Often what is really being asked is "will we see you more?" or "are you okay?". Answering that directly can settle more than a debate about plans.
* **Choose the moment.**

   A long discussion over a busy family meal rarely goes well. A one-to-one conversation on a walk or over chai usually does.
* **Allow yourself not to know.**

   "I don't know yet" is an honest answer to a decision that is still open.

## The low after you leave again

Feeling flat in the days after you fly back is common, and it usually eases.

You have just gone from a full, warm, noisy schedule to your own quiet routine, often with jet lag and a work inbox waiting. The contrast is sharp. Your own place can feel too quiet, and ordinary tasks may feel heavier than usual.

Some people also notice a second layer: a sense that the goodbye was rushed, or that they did not say or do something they meant to. The mind tends to replay the trip and search for what went wrong, which can keep the low going.

What often helps is treating the return as a planned transition rather than a drop:

* Leave a buffer day before work or classes, if you can.
* Book something small to look forward to in the first week, such as a call with family, dinner with a friend or a favourite meal.
* Keep a gentle routine: sleep, regular meals, daylight and some movement.
* Share photos and stories with someone who will enjoy them, so the trip stays alive instead of being packed away with the suitcase.

## A CBT and ACT toolkit for before, during and after

Small, specific habits work better than trying to make yourself feel a certain way.

**Before you go**

* **Set intentions, not a perfect itinerary.**

   Pick two or three things that matter most, such as a long lunch with a grandparent or one evening with old friends, and keep the rest flexible. This is the ACT idea of choosing what you want the trip to be about.
* **Leave one day unscheduled.**

   Rest is not wasted time.
* **Name your expectations.**

   Ask yourself, "What am I hoping this trip will fix or prove?" Putting it into words loosens its grip.

**While you are there**

* **Plan some time alone.**

   A walk or an hour in your room is a normal need, not a rejection of anyone.
* **Test all-or-nothing thoughts.**

   "This day was wasted" can be checked: what was good about it, even if small?
* **Be fully present for one thing a day.**

   Put the phone away for one meal and give it your full attention. It helps with the feeling of time slipping away.
* **Say the thing early.**

   If there is something you want to tell or ask someone, do it in the first few days rather than at the airport.

**When you are back**

* **Use behavioural activation.**

   Schedule small, specific, pleasant activities for your first week. Mood often follows action, not the other way round.
* **Make room for the sadness.**

   Missing people is a sign of connection. ACT suggests allowing the feeling while still doing what matters to you that day.
* **Plan the next contact.**

   A weekly call or the next trip on the calendar means the goodbye is not the end of the story.

## When to seek extra support

A few low or tense days around a trip are a normal response, but some signs suggest it is worth talking to someone.

* Low mood, irritability or loss of interest that lasts more than two weeks after you return.
* Trouble sleeping, eating or concentrating that does not settle.
* Dreading every future visit, or avoiding contact with family to escape the feelings.
* Persistent guilt, or a sense of being stuck between two lives with no way forward.

A counsellor can help you sort out which feelings belong to this trip and which have been building for longer. If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, please contact a local emergency service or crisis helpline straight away.
